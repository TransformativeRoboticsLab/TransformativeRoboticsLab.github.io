How this model works

Voxel. Each AuxSwarm voxel is a jitterbug-style auxetic shell made of 0.254 mm spring steel. Two mirror-image layers are offset by 6 mm standoffs and pinned at the midpoints of their rotating links, which forms a scissor between faces (Sec. II-A, Fig. 1). Two servos counter-rotate the layers, a single degree of freedom. The diameter grows from 92 ± 1.2 mm closed to 144 ± 1.6 mm open over 45° of servo rotation. That is k = 1.57, against a theoretical 1.77, and it takes 0.2 s (Sec. III, Fig. 2). Diameter is linearly interpolated between those two reported endpoints, which approximates the Fig. 2 curve.

Two voxels (Fig. 3A). A contracted voxel of radius r and an expanded voxel of radius kr stay tangent at their joint. The line through their centres meets the ground at θ with sin θ = (k − 1)/(k + 1) (Eq. 1), and the common tangent rises at 2θ = 2 arcsin((k − 1)/(k + 1)) (Eq. 2). That is the steepest incline one pair can make.

Bending layer (Eq. 3). When the pair is tiled by symmetry, each expanded voxel spans 2θ about a common centre, so sin θ = kr/(R − kr) and R = 2k²r/(k − 1). The paper uses r = 45.9 mm.

Simplified: the drawn shell is a 2D schematic of two counter-rotating polygon layers, not the real 3D expandohedron. The gimbal joints are treated as points of tangency, as in the paper. Gravity and contact friction are ignored. The paper notes friction limited real expansion to about 115 mm in the bending test (Sec. V).

Reported: stiffness 388 ± 41 N/m from the top and 280 ± 20 N/m laterally. Blocked force 8.92 ± 0.17 N along the servo axis and 3.02 ± 0.46 N laterally. Bending radius 63 cm with a constraint layer and 55 cm without (body length 51 cm), vs a predicted 39 cm at k = 1.57 or 57 cm at k = 1.25 (Sec. III, V).
