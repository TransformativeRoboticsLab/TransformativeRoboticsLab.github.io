How this model works

Noise sources (report Sec. 1.2, TRR abstract). Each tire drops into the gaps between the joint's steel beams, then strikes the leading edge of the next beam. That impact rings the beams and the tire. It also pumps the air cavity formed by the tire, beams and neoprene seal, which acts like a Helmholtz resonator. Measured noise peaked at 400–800 Hz, and the peak frequency fell as tire width grew (f ∝ 1/Wt).

Treatment (Secs. 2.1–2.2). The gaps are filled with chevron supports that hold the tire up. The chevrons are tailored for near-zero Poisson's ratio, so they stretch and shrink with the gap without changing height or length along the joint. They must cope with gaps from 0.84 to 3 in. in service (horizontal expansion ratio above 3.5) and still carry wheel loads.

Rigid-wheel sketch (ours). Treat the tire as a rigid circle of radius R rolling over an open gap of width w. It sinks δ = R − √(R² − (w/2)²) and meets the far edge at an angle γ = asin(w/2R). At speed v the hub must gain a vertical velocity of about v·sin γ at the strike. A flush filler removes both. A real loaded tire flattens and drapes further into the gap, so δ here is a lower bound. This sketch is not the paper's model. The paper used ANSYS FEA with a 2-layer tire, 35 psi and a 1000 lbf wheel load (Sec. 3.3).

Reported FEA (Sec. 2.5, 3-in. gap). Peak beam-edge pressure was 4.39 MPa unsupported and 1.45 MPa on a flat road. The single-layer chevron cut the excess by 67.17% and the multi-layer one by 76.40%. Support surface deflection was 3.60 mm and 2.08 mm, with peak stress 4.64 MPa and 2.91 MPa against 14.5 MPa strength for FP-90 urethane.

Field test (TRR abstract). A single-layer treatment on one westbound lane of the SR 520 floating bridge was tested for two months. It gave more than 10 dB reduction in the 500–900 Hz band at 160 ft.
