How this model works

Spring sizing (Sec. III-A). Stance is modelled as a spring-mass oscillator, m ẍ + K(x − x₀) + mg = 0. Equating the energy at the lowest point to a hop of height h gives K = 2mg(h + Δx)Δx⁻². For m = 1.3 kg and Δx = h = 5 cm, that is about 1 kN/m.

Hop frequency (Eq. 1). f ≈ (2√(2h/g) + π√(m/K))⁻¹, which is flight time plus stance time. The paper finds f(1.3, 1000) ≈ 3.2 Hz.

HSA stiffness. The 8-row, 3-column FPU50 HSA averaged 912 N/m over a 5 cm stroke at positive twist. Twisting it to about 135° jams it against a rigid inner insert (Sec. III-D), and the stiffness then exceeds 16 kN/m, 21× the lowest value (Fig. 2). Here that is reduced to two states: 912 N/m below 135° and 16 kN/m once jammed. The full Fig. 2 map is not reproduced.

Holding power (Eq. 2). Pelec = RI² + τθ̇. Holding still means θ̇ = 0, so only Joule heating RI² remains, with R = 0.143 Ω (Table II). The current comes from I = τm/Kt, with τm = F·ℓ / 6 (1:6 gear, Table I). The table's motor constant "105" is read as 105 rpm/V, giving Kt = 60/(2π·105) ≈ 0.091 N·m/A. The moment arm ℓ is assumed to be the 7 cm thigh length. Both are assumptions. When the HSA is jammed, the brake carries the load and the leg motor needs no torque. The paper measured that the twist servo's power grows only linearly with load (Fig. 4), but gives no fit, so the servo is not modelled.

Simplified: the animation is an ideal lossless spring-mass hop at the chosen height. The HSA's damping, the leg linkage and the controller are not modelled. The paper itself notes that it has no dynamic HSA model (Sec. VII-A).

Reported results: the HSA cut hopping electrical cost by 24–32% across masses. Hopping cost of transport was comparable to SPEAR (equivalent COT 2.46), and spring efficiency was η = 29% vs 64% for SPEAR's steel spring (Sec. VI–VII).
