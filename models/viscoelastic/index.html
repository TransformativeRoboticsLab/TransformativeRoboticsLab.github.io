How this model works

Printing. An inkjet printer deposits a rubbery solid (TangoBlack+) and a non-curing liquid side by side. Algorithm 2 assigns each voxel inside the part to the liquid with probability Pl, and to the solid otherwise. The right panel shows one such random cross-section with 30 × 40 µm voxels drawn enlarged.

Small strain (ε < 0.01, Sec. IV-A). DMA tests from 1 to 100 Hz showed power laws E′ = E′|1Hz·ωn1 and E″ = E″|1Hz·ωn2 (Eqs. 3–4). E′|1Hz, E″|1Hz and tan δ|1Hz follow a·ebPl + c·edPl. n1 and n2 change form at 25% liquid, where the paper suggests the material becomes an open-cell foam (Table II).

Large strain / impact (Eq. 6). e* = p₁Pl³ + p₂Pl² + p₃Pl + p₄, fitted to drop tests with 0–36% liquid. Its minimum near 4–10% liquid is the most energy-absorbing mix. Successive bounce heights scale as e*².

Isolation (Eqs. 7–10). The puck is a complex spring K* = (A₀/L₀)E′|1Hz(ωn1 + i·tan δ|1Hz·ωn2) (Eq. 8). It carries a mass m on a vibrating base.

Our choices and simplifications. (1) In Eqs. 3–5, ω is read as frequency in Hz normalised by 1 Hz, so the moduli equal their 1 Hz values at 1 Hz. The inertia term uses ω = 2πf. (2) Eq. 10 is printed as K*/(mω² + K*). We use the standard base-excitation form K*/(K* − mω²) so that a resonance can appear. (3) Above 100 Hz, and outside 0–36% liquid for e*, the fits are extrapolated, and this is flagged. (4) The peak force readout is our own linear-spring estimate, F ≈ v·√(m·K′|1Hz). It is not a model from the paper. The paper reports measured peak forces in Fig. 2b and a ≈700 N drop from 0% to 25% liquid. (5) In the drop animation the contact phase is slowed down further and is not to time scale.

Reported robot result (Sec. V-A, Fig. 4): an 18% liquid skin roughly halved the jumping cube's peak landing acceleration compared with a cast foam skin, and reduced bouncing.
