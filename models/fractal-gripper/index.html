Fractal suction gripper

Illustrative model based on the abstract of O'Brien, Kowalewski, Kessens & Lipton, A Fractal Suction-Based Robotic Gripper for Versatile Grasping (IEEE RA-L 2024). The full text was not available to us, so this is a simplified 2D model of the key idea, not the paper's model. Default values are placeholders, not the paper's.

Suction cups sit at the tips of a branching tree of free pivots, like a whiffletree. Each pivot rotates until both of its branches touch, so the cups spread over curved surfaces and corners and stay square to the surface over a wider range of approach angles than one rigid cup.

How this model works

Simplified mechanics, stated plainly
  • What comes from the paper (abstract only): a fractal tree of passive joints makes a suction gripper more conformable and widens the approach-angle range. The authors report 5° for a standard suction gripper and 45° for theirs, enabled corner grasping, and found that vacuum-actuated joint brakes improved performance by over 30% at moderate contact angles. Those are the paper's measured results. This demo does not reproduce them.
  • Geometry (this demo): a planar binary tree. Level i has half-span s0·ki and drop h. Every bar hangs from a frictionless pivot that can turn ±φmax relative to its parent bar, and the last bars carry the cups rigidly.
  • Conforming: the gripper is pushed straight in along its axis. Working from the leaves up, each pivot takes the angle that lets both branches touch, sin φ = (z1 − z2)/(2s), clipped at ±φmax. A few fixed-point passes update branch positions as the bars rotate. The gripper stops at the first contact.
  • Seal rule: a cup seals if its residual gap is no more than the lip allowance and its face is within ±αc of the local surface normal. αc = 5° echoes the single-cup range in the abstract. Set n = 0 to see one rigid cup.
  • Holding force (simplified): a pull along the gripper axis splits equally at each pivot whose two branches both hold a seal, or goes entirely to the one branch that does. Each sealed cup holds until ΔP·A is reached by the normal component, or by the shear component divided by μs = 0.5. The payload is the smallest cup capacity divided by that cup's share. Joint brakes are not modelled.
  • Chart: sealed cups and model holding force against approach angle ψ for the current surface, compared with a single rigid cup. These are model curves, not measurements.
